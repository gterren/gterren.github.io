## Teaching philosophy

My teaching philosophy centers on the ability to ask meaningful questions, which I believe is
the most valuable skill a student can develop. I encourage students to embrace the scientific
method: formulating hypotheses, identifying the information needed to evaluate them, and
determining whether the answer already exists in the literature. When it does not, I guide them
in identifying the data, methods, and analytical tools to investigate the problem, so that they
become independent in searching for, validating, and applying new knowledge.

## Course design, assessment, and AI

I design courses that encourage multiple forms of engagement, combining lecture-based
instruction, hands-on activities, and project-based learning. For foundational material I use an
incremental approach, with low-stakes quizzes and in-class exercises that help students retain
concepts and reduce the pressure of high-stakes exams. For open-ended problems I assign
collaborative projects that begin with students formulating the question before they answer it,
which develops technical problem-solving, experience with shared workflows and version control,
and clear technical communication. I keep expectations and rubrics explicit from the first day
while varying the forms of assessment, so that students of different backgrounds and preparation
can all succeed.

Because I have studied and built planning models for different electricity systems, I understand
how practices around capacity expansion, planning reserve margins, electricity markets, and
ancillary services differ from one system to another. In my teaching I ensure that students learn
the underlying principles rather than simply following local standards, and where possible I
present alternative approaches.

I also address the growing role of artificial intelligence in learning environments. I frame AI as
a resource that can support brainstorming, coding, and improving clarity in writing, while
emphasizing that it should not replace students' own reasoning, originality, or responsibility for
their work. I teach students to use AI critically by cross-checking claims against academic
literature and technical documentation, verifying citations and data sources, testing
computational outputs for robustness, and being transparent about when and how they used it.

## Mentoring

As a first-generation college student, I understand the uncertainty that can accompany navigating
unfamiliar academic systems. I have mentored one postdoctoral scholar, three graduate students,
and five undergraduate students.

I help graduate students at CETlab sharpen research questions and present their ideas at seminars,
oral exams, and defenses. I trained a postdoctoral scholar to use GridPath-India, and he went on
to develop the workflow for the resource adequacy study now in preparation. This progression from
guided onboarding to research independence reflects the approach I hope to foster in my own group.

In the internships I have supervised, I define a project within the student's reach but beyond
their comfort zone, starting from a conversation about their background, interests, and prior
experience. One student built a Python workflow to process time series from over 300 wind sites
and 500 solar sites, identifying seasonal and spatial patterns and quantifying uncertainties, and
presented the results at the SACNAS National Diversity in STEM Conference. Another designed and
built a circuit board to process solar radiation measurements, powered over USB and transmitting
data to a computer.
